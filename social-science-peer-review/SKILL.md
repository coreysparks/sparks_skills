# Social Science Peer Review

## Overview

Structured peer review for social science manuscripts. 7-stage evaluation: initial assessment, section-by-section review, methodological rigor, reproducibility, tables/figures, ethics, and writing quality. Covers causal inference, survey methodology, multilevel models, qualitative methods. Includes reporting standards (APA, JARS, SRQR, COREQ) and review report structure.

Peer review for social science manuscripts requires evaluating theoretical contribution, methodological rigor, and substantive interpretation. This skill covers the complete review cycle: initial assessment through detailed evaluation of research design, statistical analysis, qualitative methods, reproducibility, and writing — culminating in a structured review report with actionable feedback for demography, sociology, economics, political science, public health, and related fields.

## Key Concepts

### 1. Review Types and Expectations

| Review Type | Scope | Typical Length | Key Focus |
|-------------|-------|---------------|-----------|
| **Empirical quantitative** | Full evaluation | 2–4 pages | Causal identification, measurement, statistical rigor |
| **Empirical qualitative** | Methods + interpretation | 2–3 pages | Sampling, saturation, reflexivity, trustworthiness |
| **Mixed methods** | Integration assessment | 2–4 pages | Complementarity, integration quality, joint inference |
| **Theoretical/conceptual** | Argument structure | 1–3 pages | Internal logic, literature engagement, contribution |
| **Systematic review** | Coverage and synthesis | 2–3 pages | Search strategy, inclusion criteria, synthesis method |
| **Policy brief/commentary** | Proportional | 1–2 pages | Evidence basis, clarity of implications |

### 2. Comment Severity Levels

- **Major comments**: Issues affecting validity, interpretability, or contribution. Must be addressed for publication. Examples: identification strategy flaws, unsupported causal claims, unacknowledged selection bias, missing theoretical grounding.
- **Minor comments**: Issues improving clarity or completeness without affecting core validity. Examples: unclear variable operationalization, missing descriptive statistics, presentation improvements.
- **Questions for authors**: Clarification requests where evaluation depends on additional information.

### 3. Reporting Standards for Social Science

| Standard | Applies to | Key Checks |
|----------|-----------|-----------|
| **APA Style (7th ed.)** | Most social science | Tables, figures, statistics reporting, effect sizes |
| **JARS-Quant** | Quantitative research | Hypotheses, participants, measures, analysis plan |
| **JARS-Qual** | Qualitative research | Research design, researcher description, data collection |
| **SRQR** | Qualitative studies | 21-item checklist: reflexivity, context, data adequacy |
| **COREQ** | Interviews/focus groups | 32-item checklist: team, methods, analysis, findings |
| **STROBE** | Observational epidemiology | Study design, participants, variables, bias discussion |
| **PRISMA** | Systematic reviews | Search strategy, PICO, risk of bias, synthesis |
| **CONSORT-SPI** | Social/psychological interventions | Randomization, fidelity, contamination |

### 4. Methodological Concerns by Design

| Design | Primary Threats | Key Questions |
|--------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Cross-sectional survey** | Selection, reverse causality, confounding | Sampling frame? Response rate? Weighting? |
| **Panel/longitudinal** | Attrition, time-varying confounders | Attrition analysis? Fixed effects justified? |
| **Quasi-experimental** | Selection into treatment, parallel trends | Pre-trends shown? Placebo tests? Bandwidth sensitivity? |
| **RCT/experiment** | Compliance, spillover, external validity | ITT vs. LATE? Randomization verified? SUTVA? |
| **Qualitative** | Credibility, transferability, reflexivity | Saturation reached? Member checking? Thick description? |
| **Administrative data** | Measurement error, missing by design | Linkage quality? Coverage gaps? Construct validity? |
| **Multilevel/hierarchical** | Clustering, small N at higher levels | ICC reported? Level-2 N adequate? Random slopes? |

## Decision Framework

```
What type of social science paper?
├── Empirical quantitative
│   ├── Causal claim → Focus on identification strategy, confounding, selection
│   ├── Descriptive/predictive → Focus on measurement, generalizability, precision
│   └── Methodological → Focus on validation, comparison to alternatives
├── Empirical qualitative
│   └── Focus on sampling logic, data adequacy, interpretation grounding
├── Mixed methods
│   └── Focus on integration rationale, how components inform each other
├── Theoretical
│   └── Focus on internal logic, literature engagement, conceptual contribution
└── Review/meta-analysis
    └── Focus on search completeness, synthesis method, heterogeneity
```

| Reviewer Situation | Focus Areas | Time Budget |
|-------------------|-------------|-------------|
| First-round journal review | All 7 stages, full detail | 4–8 hours |
| Revision re-review | Check if previous concerns addressed | 1–2 hours |
| Internal feedback (pre-submission) | Theory, methods, results (skip reporting standards) | 2–4 hours |
| Conference paper | Abbreviated: contribution, methods overview | 1–2 hours |
| Working paper/preprint | Focus on core methodology, interpretation | 2–4 hours |

## Best Practices

1. **Read completely before annotating**: Your first-pass impression distinguishes major from minor issues. Note the paper's stated contribution before evaluating whether it delivers.

2. **Evaluate the paper's own claims, not your preferred design**: A well-executed descriptive study should not be faulted for lacking causal identification if it makes no causal claims. Judge whether methods support stated conclusions.

3. **Separate description from judgment**: State what you observed ("The model includes only year fixed effects"), why it matters ("This does not account for region-specific trends that could confound the treatment effect"), and suggest a fix ("Add region-by-year trends or show parallel pre-trends").

4. **Number every comment**: Sequential numbering lets authors address each point explicitly in their response.

5. **State confidence on methodological concerns**: "I am uncertain whether the IV exclusion restriction holds here—the authors should provide evidence that [instrument] affects [outcome] only through [treatment]."

6. **Engage with theory**: Social science papers should connect empirical findings to broader theoretical frameworks. Note when theoretical motivation is missing, underdeveloped, or disconnected from the analysis.

7. **Check for selection and confounding systematically**: For every causal claim, ask: "What determines who receives treatment? Could those same factors affect the outcome directly?"

8. **Evaluate heterogeneity and external validity**: Who is in the sample? Are effects likely to generalize? Are subgroup analyses exploratory or confirmatory? Is multiple testing addressed?

9. **Acknowledge strengths explicitly**: Every review should note 2–3 specific strengths. This signals to editors what should NOT change and helps authors understand what works.

10. **Match recommendation to evidence**: Minor comments → accept or minor revision. Unresolvable design flaws → reject. Major but addressable concerns → major revision.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Demanding causal identification from descriptive work**: Not every paper needs an identification strategy. Descriptive work documenting patterns, trends, or associations has independent value if framed appropriately. *How to avoid*: Check what claims the authors actually make before criticizing identification.

2. **Ignoring the theoretical contribution**: Social science papers contribute through theory, not just empirics. A paper with modest empirical novelty may offer substantial theoretical insight. *How to avoid*: Evaluate theory and empirics as separate dimensions.

3. **Asymmetric skepticism**: Scrutinizing findings that challenge your priors more than those that confirm them. *How to avoid*: Apply identical methodological standards regardless of whether you agree with conclusions.

4. **Conflating statistical and substantive significance**: A p < 0.001 result can be trivially small; a p = 0.08 result can be substantively important. *How to avoid*: Always evaluate effect sizes and practical implications, not just p-values.

5. **Requesting a different study**: Suggesting the authors collect new data, use a different sample, or run additional waves is beyond revision scope. *How to avoid*: Frame limitations for the Discussion rather than as revision requirements. Suggest as future work.

6. **Overlooking survey methodology**: For survey-based research, ignoring sampling design, nonresponse, and weighting in favor of focusing only on regression specification. *How to avoid*: Check: sampling frame, response rate, weighting, and whether standard errors account for clustering.

7. **Treating qualitative research as "soft" quantitative**: Applying quantitative validity concepts (sample size, generalizability) inappropriately to qualitative work. *How to avoid*: Use qualitative-specific criteria: credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability.

8. **Missing the mechanism**: Accepting that X affects Y without asking how or why. *How to avoid*: Does the paper theorize a mechanism? Is there evidence for it? Could an alternative mechanism explain the same pattern?

## Workflow

### 7-Stage Social Science Review Process

**Stage 1: Initial Assessment** (read without annotation)
- Identify the central research question and stated contribution
- Note the theoretical framework and disciplinary positioning
- Assess overall soundness and venue appropriateness
- Write 2–3 sentence summary capturing the paper's essence

**Stage 2: Section-by-Section Review**

*Abstract*
- Does it accurately summarize question, data, methods, findings, and implications?
- Are causal claims appropriately hedged?

*Introduction*
- Is the research question clearly stated and motivated?
- Is the theoretical framework articulated?
- Is the contribution positioned relative to existing literature?
- Is the "gap" genuine, or manufactured by ignoring relevant work?

*Literature Review / Theory*
- Is coverage adequate and balanced?
- Are key debates and prior findings accurately characterized?
- Does the theory generate testable hypotheses or clear expectations?
- Is the paper's contribution clearly distinguished from prior work?

*Data and Methods*
- Is the data source appropriate for the research question?
- Are variables operationalized clearly and justified theoretically?
- Is the sample adequately described (size, characteristics, selection)?
- Is the identification strategy or analytic approach appropriate?
- Are assumptions stated and justified?
- Is there enough detail to reproduce the analysis?

*Results*
- Are results presented clearly and completely?
- Are tables and figures interpretable without excessive text?
- Is statistical reporting complete (effect sizes, CIs, appropriate tests)?
- Are sensitivity analyses and robustness checks adequate?
- Is there evidence of p-hacking or selective reporting?

*Discussion*
- Are interpretations supported by the results?
- Are limitations honestly acknowledged?
- Is speculation clearly flagged as such?
- Are theoretical and policy implications appropriate given the evidence?
- Does the paper overreach its contribution?

**Stage 3: Methodological Rigor**

*For quantitative research:*
- Identification strategy: What is the source of variation? Is it exogenous?
- Selection and confounding: What determines treatment/exposure? Controlled for?
- Measurement: Are constructs validly operationalized? Reliability?
- Statistical assumptions: Linearity, independence, distributional assumptions met?
- Multiple comparisons: How many tests? Correction applied?
- Sample size and power: Adequate to detect plausible effects?
- Missing data: How much? Mechanism (MCAR/MAR/MNAR)? Handling?
- Clustering: Are standard errors appropriate for data structure?

*For qualitative research:*
- Sampling: Is the sampling strategy appropriate and clearly described?
- Data adequacy: Is there evidence of saturation or sufficient depth?
- Reflexivity: Does the researcher acknowledge their position and influence?
- Analysis: Is the analytic process transparent and systematic?
- Interpretation: Are claims grounded in the data? Negative cases addressed?
- Trustworthiness: Member checking, triangulation, audit trail?

**Stage 4: Reproducibility and Transparency**
- Data: Available? Archived? Access restrictions explained?
- Code/syntax: Provided? Documented? Version controlled?
- Pre-registration: Was the study pre-registered? Deviations noted?
- Materials: Survey instruments, interview protocols available?
- Reporting standards: Does the paper follow relevant guidelines (JARS, SRQR, STROBE)?

**Stage 5: Tables and Figures**
- Are tables formatted according to journal/APA standards?
- Are figure labels, axes, and legends clear and complete?
- Are uncertainty measures (SEs, CIs) consistently reported?
- Are effect sizes interpretable (standardized coefficients, marginal effects)?
- Is color accessibility considered?
- Do supplementary materials contain necessary detail?

**Stage 6: Ethical Considerations**
- IRB/ethics approval documented?
- Informed consent obtained appropriately?
- Vulnerable populations protected?
- Data privacy and confidentiality addressed?
- Conflicts of interest disclosed?
- Funding sources disclosed?
- Positionality addressed (especially for qualitative work)?

**Stage 7: Writing Quality**
- Is the prose clear and accessible to the intended audience?
- Is jargon minimized or defined?
- Is the paper appropriately structured for the discipline?
- Is the length appropriate (neither padded nor underdeveloped)?
- Are citations complete and accurate?

### Review Report Structure

**Summary Statement** (2–3 paragraphs)
- Synopsis of the paper's question, approach, and main findings
- Overall assessment and recommendation
- 2–3 specific strengths
- 2–3 main concerns (previewing major comments)

**Major Comments** (numbered)
- Issues affecting validity, interpretability, or contribution
- For each: observation → why it matters → suggested resolution
- Focus on what authors can actually address in revision

**Minor Comments** (numbered)
- Clarity, completeness, presentation improvements
- Specific locations (page, table, figure numbers)

**Questions for Authors**
- Clarification requests that affect evaluation
- Distinguish from rhetorical questions in major/minor comments

**Recommendation**
- Accept / Minor revision / Major revision / Reject
- Must be consistent with the severity of identified issues

## Discipline-Specific Considerations

### Demography
- Age-period-cohort identification
- Life table methods and survival analysis
- Population projection assumptions
- Survey weighting and complex designs (DHS, census)
- Decomposition methods

### Sociology
- Theory-data connection
- Mechanisms and process
- Qualitative rigor standards
- Mixed methods integration
- Intersectionality and heterogeneity

### Economics
- Identification strategy rigor
- Instrument validity and exclusion restrictions
- Structural vs. reduced-form interpretation
- Welfare implications and policy counterfactuals
- External validity and LATE interpretation

### Political Science
- Case selection and comparative method
- Measurement of latent concepts (democracy, corruption)
- Causal inference in observational settings
- Survey experiments and conjoint analysis
- Text-as-data methods

### Public Health / Epidemiology
- STROBE compliance for observational studies
- DAGs and confounder selection
- Mediation and interaction
- Population attributable fractions
- Health equity considerations
